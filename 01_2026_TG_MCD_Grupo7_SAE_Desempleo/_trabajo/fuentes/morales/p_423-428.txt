In practice, it may occur that varπ(yd) = 0 for some domains d ∈D0 ⊂D = {1, . . ., D}. This is quite common if the unit-level target variable, ydj, is dichotomic. In those cases, log varπ(yd) = −∞and the GVF method is not applicable. A simple solution is to add a very small quantity (e.g. ϵ = 10−6) to the zero estimated variances. Alternatively, the GVF method can be applied to the subset of domains D1 = D −D0 where varπ(yd) > 0. For the domains d ∈D0, the GVF estimates can be ﬁxed to ˆσ 2 GV F,d = ε · min 6 ˆσ 2 GV F,d : d ∈D1 7 , 0 < ε < 1, d ∈D0. The GVF method makes a smoothing of the estimates of the design-based variances. Applied statisticians should try not to do over- or under-smoothing. On the one hand, the GVF model (16.6) does not need to give accurate predictions of varπ(yd), d = 1, . . ., D. We use this model when we do not trust the design- based estimates varπ(yd) of error variances σ 2 d , because the direct estimators of variances give bad estimates when domain sample sizes are small. In some problems of regression models, the dependent variable is measured without error. In the case of the GVF model, it is not like that. Therefore, we are not interested in obtaining a model with high predictive capacity. We only want to make a smoothing of the estimates of the design-based variances of the direct estimators of target quantities. We prefer a model having a reasonable interpretation. On the other hand, a model with low predictive capacity may produce an over- smoothing of the variance estimates. To avoid this problem, it might be convenient to include in the GVF model (16.6) some new explanatory variables, for example, y2 d, n2 d, ndyd, or some component of xd with p-values greater than 0.05. By doing so, the smoothing problem will be reduced or suppressed. This is a parsimonious solution if the new auxiliary variables are interpretable.
